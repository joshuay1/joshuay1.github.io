From Voices to Shared Understanding: AI-Supported Dynamic Participatory Sensemaking in Citizen Deliberations

Joshua C. Yang, Maud Reveilhac, Aurelia Tamò-Larrieux, Dirk Helbing, and Kenneth Holstein

Preprint · September 2026 · Under review at CHI

Read the paper (PDF)

Abstract

Citizen deliberation offers people with different experiences and priorities a way to shape responses to public problems together. Yet power to shape deliberation outputs often remains in the hands of a select few. Advances in AI offer potential to enable more participatory modes of summarisation, where participants co-construct evolving summaries during live deliberations. We developed Murmi to explore the potential for AI-supported dynamic participatory sensemaking, by integrating live discussion, voting on summarised position cards, and contestation of summaries. Across four workshops, questionnaire responses from 76 participants more often favoured the AI-supported workflow for perceived output accuracy (51% versus 38% for human moderation), but favoured a human-moderated workflow for feeling heard. Participants valued opportunities to express views and question wording, emphasis and missing concerns. These findings inform the design of AI-supported deliberation in which citizens shape not only the discussion, but how their collective views are interpreted and represented.

1 Introduction

Democratic participation holds the promise that people with different experiences and priorities can shape their shared future together. Citizen deliberation creates space to consider what they share, where they disagree and what they might do together [3, 13]. Realising this promise depends partly on how people’s contributions are heard, interpreted and carried forward. AI opens possibilities for involving citizens in this process by connecting ongoing discussion with voting and summary review. We explore what these possibilities mean in practice through dynamic participatory sensemaking in live citizen deliberation.

Voting gives participants another way to respond to one another’s views alongside spoken exchange [29, 34]. It can help focus discussion and preserve dissent amid pressures for consensus [7]. During a live conversation, these responses could identify ideas that need further explanation or discussion. Yet a vote responds to a statement as it has been worded; the reasons, conditions or uncertainty behind a position may be harder to express. The challenge is how voting can enrich the conversation while leaving room for people to clarify what they mean.

HCI research has explored AI-supported discussion and collaborative summarisation [8, 30, 33]. Pol.is lets participants contribute statements and respond to those of others, making patterns of agreement and difference visible [29]. In processes such as vTaiwan, these patterns inform deliberation that connects online participation with discussions among citizens and stakeholders [29, 35]. We examine how spoken contributions, voting and summary review can remain connected during a live citizen workshop. Participants can help shape the voting cards and summaries while using them to identify what needs further discussion.

Work on participatory sense-making examines how meaning develops through interaction between people [11]. In a workshop, a participant might notice that a voting card has left out a condition attached to their proposal and explain why it matters. This can prompt clearer wording and further discussion. We use dynamic participatory sensemaking to describe this continuing relationship between discussion and how contributions are represented in cards and summaries. Participants interpret these materials together, and their responses can shape both the wording and the conversation that follows. AI helps prepare the materials, while participants and moderators draw on their experience of the exchange to question what they contain. Participation therefore includes having a say in how views are understood and brought together.

To explore this process, we developed Murmi. The platform transcribed discussion and extracted propositions for moderators to check before presenting them as voting cards. Participants indicated their positions through brief judgements, or micro-votes, added statements of their own and reviewed summaries generated from propositions and responses. The design aimed to help them identify what needed further discussion (Figures 1 and 2).

Whether these opportunities become part of a shared conversation depends on how people use them. Reading cards and responding to them also take attention during discussion. Moderators decided which extracted propositions reached participants. Their screen was projected so participants could follow and question edits. We examine how overlapping discussion, voting and review worked in practice, including what participants valued and how requests for changes were handled.

We studied four language-group workshops across two Swiss events and three languages. Each room followed the human-moderated workflow and then the AI-supported workflow. Questionnaires from 76 participants and workshop records let us examine their experiences alongside exchanges in which people questioned or tried to change cards and summaries. We ask:

  1. How did participants assess the discussion outputs and their opportunities to contribute and deliberate in the human-moderated and AI-supported workflows?

  2. How did participants and moderators work with voting cards and summaries during discussion, and what did they accept, question or seek to change?

We make three contributions to understanding dynamic participatory sensemaking in live citizen workshops. First, we trace how contributions were represented and contested across transcripts, voting cards, responses, summaries and reports. Second, we identify the articulation work of participant review: checking wording, explaining omissions and coordinating changes while discussion continues. Third, we derive design implications for connecting discussion, voting and review: making sources and revisions visible, providing time to consider and correct cards and summaries together, and distinguishing support for propositions from acceptance of a summary and authorisation of a public report.

2 Related Work

We draw on research on how people make sense of discussion with others, how voting relates to deliberation, and how participants review summaries of a group’s views.

2.1 Making sense of discussion together

De Jaegher and Di Paolo examine how meaning develops and changes as people respond to one another [11]. We use dynamic participatory sensemaking to examine this continuing process when contributions return as AI-generated cards and summaries. Participants can question and revise the cards and summaries, then return to discussing the ideas they contain.

Collaborative systems already support this relationship between discussion and representation. Wikum lets people recursively summarise discussions, while Wikum+ interweaves discussion and summarisation so that summaries can develop as contributions and feedback arrive [33, 36]. MeetMap examines real-time dialogue mapping in online meetings. In a study with ten pairs, participants valued the opportunity to construct maps from AI-generated nodes, as well as the lower effort of editing maps generated by AI [8]. In each system, participants help organise the discussion, though the opportunities to do so and the effort required differ.

AI can also affect how people encounter one another’s contributions. Research on AI-mediated communication examines systems that modify or generate communication on a person’s behalf [15]; Argyle and colleagues studied suggested rephrasings during political conversations [2]. Research on collaborative programming similarly examines how AI assistance enters collaboration between peers [10]. Revel and Pénigaud propose AI as a reflector that supports collective sensemaking and agreement-seeking without making binding decisions [27].

Collaborative summarisation also requires people to coordinate their work. Articulation work concerns organising interdependent activities [28], including who prepares a summary, who checks it and who responds when someone asks for a change. Such effort may be less visible than the finished output [31]. In a live workshop, people need time for these tasks alongside speaking and listening.

Our study extends this work on collaborative summarisation and dialogue mapping by examining how participants and moderators work through the meaning of AI-generated cards and summaries during live citizen discussion. We trace exchanges in which people clarify a contribution, question its representation and coordinate changes. The observations show what participants and moderators do when they interpret and revise cards and summaries during discussion.

2.2 Connecting voting with live deliberation

Deliberation and voting offer complementary ways to participate, while their combination raises questions about how positions relate to reasons and discussion [7, 34]. ConsiderIt connects positions with participant-authored pro and con points [20], and PolicyCraft supports discussion and voting on concrete cases [21]. Pol.is gathers participant statements and responses to map patterns of agreement and difference [29]. In vTaiwan, opinion mapping forms part of a process connecting online participation with deliberation among citizens and stakeholders [29, 35]. In these settings, voting accompanies opportunities to consider other people’s contributions.

Prior work has combined opinion mapping with spectrum-based voting to make positions visible before and after discussion, while identifying fixed statement wording as a limitation when ideas evolve during deliberation [35].

Research on AI-augmented deliberation has explored support for several activities, including structuring contributions, assisting facilitators and developing collective statements. BCause presents an architecture for transforming transcripts into structured arguments that moderators can curate and use in reports [1]. In a Finnish Citizens’ Assembly, Jäske and colleagues reported that AI eased the workload of facilitators and deliberators in co-creation and integration, but encountered shortcomings when creating questions for experts [18]. Konya and colleagues combined participant contributions and voting, AI-assisted generation of collective statements, multilingual human review and final votes among Israeli and Palestinian peacebuilders. The resulting statements received at least 84% agreement within each participating group [19].

Building on approaches that connect participant statements, voting and collective outputs, we developed and studied a workflow in which spoken contributions can become voting cards while discussion continues. Our field study examines how participants respond to new voting cards while also speaking and listening. It shows how additional ways to express a position can be valued even when participants want more shared discussion.

2.3 Judging and shaping a discussion summary

Generating statements that participants endorse and summarising a group’s discussion involve related but different judgements. Building on work on generating statements that accommodate diverse preferences [4], Tessler and colleagues’ Habermas Machine generates group statements from written individual opinions and revises them using participants’ critiques. In its human-mediator comparison, participants preferred the AI-generated statements to those written by other study participants trained for the mediator role [32]. Small and colleagues likewise propose returning AI-generated summaries to participants for checking [30]. These approaches make review part of producing a summary.

Support for separate statements does not by itself settle how they should be brought together. Judgement aggregation examines problems that arise when votes on connected propositions are combined [23], while work on textual aggregation considers criteria for representing diverse views in generated outcomes [14]. Writing a summary also involves deciding which claims to emphasise and how to connect them. Research on under-representation in group summaries examines whose perspectives remain visible after these choices [37]. Participants therefore need to judge both the individual propositions and how the summary represents the group.

Participants may also have information that the system lacks. Holstein and colleagues’ work on perceptual complementarity examines how people draw on information unavailable to an AI model. Their experiment found that prompts about such information changed how people combined it with model outputs, without necessarily improving prediction performance [17]. In a workshop, participants can draw on their experience of the discussion when explaining what a card or summary misses. Meeting-extraction research likewise illustrates difficulties in recovering context and intent from spoken interaction [24].

Review also raises questions about who can shape the summary and what their approval means. Boswell describes the difficulty of turning diverse contributions into recommendations under time pressure [6]. McKinney argues for participant verification and contextualisation of AI-generated material in citizens’ assemblies [25], while work on participatory algorithmic governance connects involvement with influence over decisions [22]. Moderator approval of a card, participant agreement with it, acceptance of a summary and permission to publish a report on behalf of the group need to be distinguished. Studying review therefore requires attention to what participants were asked to judge and what happened when they objected.

Favourable experiences are also distinct from deliberative outcomes. In a charity allocation task, Parisi and colleagues found that participants preferred LLM facilitation even though measured consensus and participation equality did not significantly improve [26]. We therefore read participants’ assessments alongside records of contributions, voting and review. This lets us examine how people used the opportunities available, without treating favourable ratings as evidence of improved deliberation.

Our study adds empirical evidence about the relationship between endorsing individual statements and accepting a summary of the group’s discussion. We compare proposition responses with summary-review responses and examine participants’ explanations of missing concerns, emphasis and uncertainty. The findings show that support for statements can coexist with rejection of the summary, while acceptance of a group summary can accompany personal disagreement with its recommendations.

3 Study Design and Methods

We developed the platform for use in citizen deliberation, with support from civic groups. Its design drew on vTaiwan’s approach to connecting online opinion gathering with face-to-face discussion [29, 35].

We conducted a participatory field study within a citizen consultation, examining participants’ experiences and the handling of contributions in human-moderated and AI-supported workflows. We combined qualitative interpretation of comments and workshop records with questionnaire comparisons and descriptive activity analyses. Research-team members helped organise and facilitate the workshops and develop the platform. Their involvement informed our understanding of its use and meant that our own decisions shaped the setting we studied.

We ran four language-group workshops across two events. Each workshop included two topic discussions, each with a 45-minute time limit. The first followed the human-moderated workflow, using sticky notes and a shared board. The second followed the AI-supported workflow, using live transcription, voting cards and summary review (Figure 3). This sequence was chosen to familiarise participants with the workshop process before they used the platform. The study examined participants’ experiences of the two workflows and how contributions were handled during the AI-supported workflow. Fixed ordering and differences in topic and discussion structure preclude isolating the effect of AI; language, group size and moderator staffing also varied across rooms.

3.1 Workshop setting and topics

The workshops formed part of a consultation on AI in education and AI at work in August 2026. Each event used two separate rooms: English and German in Zurich, and English and French in Lausanne. We refer to each language-group session as a workshop. Participants stayed in the same room for both discussions. The English rooms discussed education using the human-moderated workflow and work using the AI-supported workflow. The German and French rooms reversed this topic assignment (Table 2). No discussion material was imported from the preceding online study.

A card displays a proposition for response. Participants reviewed the physical board or an AI-generated summary as a representation of the discussion. The platform’s finalised room report combined the AI summary with the propositions and responses available at that point. Researchers subsequently interpreted the workshop material for a separate citizen workshop report.

3.2 Human-moderated and AI-supported workflows

Both workflows began with small-group discussion so participants could formulate their ideas and have an opportunity to speak before sharing with the room. In the human-moderated workflow, groups of five or six organised sticky notes into recommendations, conditions or red lines, and disagreements. They then presented their views to the room. Moderators grouped and labelled contributions on a shared board, invited corrections and asked whether it fairly represented the discussion. Researchers photographed and digitised the board. No per-note agreement counts were recorded, so a note’s presence does not establish group support.

Each Zurich room had two moderators and each Lausanne room one, with unchanged staffing across formats. Moderators guided conversation and managed the board or platform. They did not submit participant responses.

During the room-wide discussion in the AI-supported workflow, participants spoke into a shared Bluetooth microphone, and the platform transcribed speech and extracted candidate propositions. Because audio-extracted cards were created through the moderator’s device, only the moderator could edit them. Moderators could also reject, hold or approve these cards before they appeared in the Murmi app. Review was intended to preserve meaning and avoid repetitive voting. The workshop lead recalls that moderators approved most extracted statements and helped edit wording that did not adequately reflect what a participant had said. The moderator’s interface was projected onto a large screen, showing propositions and response counts (Figure 4). Participants could follow edits on the screen. If an extracted card did not reflect their intended meaning, they could raise their hand and ask the moderator to change its wording or add what was missing.

Participants responded on a five-point scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. They could also type a proposition or speak a missing view for the platform to process. Participants could directly edit cards they had submitted themselves; changes to audio-extracted cards went through the moderator. The workshop lead recalls that Zurich German participants were initially encouraged to enter statements manually before the spoken round. The platform could also generate tension cards, statements intended to bring apparent differences in responses into discussion.

Near the end of the discussion, the platform generated a draft summary from approved propositions and responses. Participants were told to select “good enough” if they were satisfied with the summary and “not good enough” if they found it unacceptable. They could also explain their concerns. The question concerned the acceptability of the summary as a whole. Participants did not vote separately on each recommendation. We used approval by at least 80% of those who reviewed the summary as a practical threshold for ending review without another iteration. Further discussion could precede a moderator-requested revision within the 45-minute limit for the topic. Lausanne French remained below the threshold after its second summary, but the time limit prevented another iteration. Participants saw the finalised room report before completing the post-discussion questionnaire.

3.3 Value-sensitive design, ethics, and participants

The platform and workshop procedures sought to protect privacy, faithful representation and participants’ ability to question cards and summaries. These values could pull in different directions: making discussion available for inspection could help people check a summary while retaining more of what they said.

The ETH Zurich Ethics Commission approved the protocol (project 26 ETHICS-116) and subsequent amendment before fieldwork. Participants gave written informed consent to live transcription and generative processing, with restricted retention of transcripts and no project audio file retained. They could choose an equally compensated human-only route. Audio was processed transiently, and the approved European-processing configuration excluded provider training and retention. Research text was access-restricted. Moderators could see aggregate votes; individual votes were hidden from them. Publications use aggregate results and short anonymised excerpts without personal identifiers. These measures limited exposure without guaranteeing anonymity: people could recognise one another’s contributions in the room, and retained text could contain identifying context. Not retaining audio also meant that later checks of transcription and interpretation had to rely on the available text.

Moderator review, projected edits and participant contributions were intended to preserve meaning and make generated wording open to challenge. Participants could question cards and reject summaries, allowing further discussion and revision.

Moderator choices still shaped wording and inclusion, and opportunities to object did not guarantee that a change preserved the intended meaning. We examine what happened when participants used those opportunities. Section 5.3 discusses moderator discretion and safeguards; the study does not establish that review prevented bias or manipulation.

Recruitment combined public outreach and a university participant pool, and was open to adults living in Switzerland who could participate in a workshop language. Participation was voluntary and compensation did not depend on expressed views.

The questionnaire analysis includes 76 respondents. Citizens were encouraged to complete the demographic questionnaire before the workshops, but completion was not required for attendance. Some attended without completing it, limiting the demographic information available for the workshop sample. Using study codes, we could match 51 workshop questionnaire respondents to demographic and AI-use information collected online beforehand (Table 2). This matched subset was relatively young, educated and familiar with AI. Of the 51 participants, 34 were under 35, 39 held a university degree and 42 used AI tools at least weekly. Mean ages were estimated from age bands.

Counting app activity.

We count votes on cards and summary-review responses separately. Summary approval is the share of review responses marked “good enough”. App activity was recorded by account or device, so these counts may not correspond to distinct participants. We used anonymous identifiers to link votes and reviews, excluding host activity from these analyses. Appendix A.1 gives the counts used in each analysis.

3.4 Platform workflow and report production

The summary model received approved propositions, response counts, voting-pattern groups, jointly supported proposition pairs and available anonymous feedback on earlier summaries. It did not receive the full discussion transcript. Its instructions asked it to retain unresolved concerns and avoid treating one person’s suggestion as group agreement. Discussion, voting and review sometimes overlapped, allowing source material to change while a summary was being reviewed.

The platform checked joint support for pairs of propositions before suggesting that the summary model combine them: separate majorities can reflect different supporting coalitions [23]. The project-specific thresholds guided generation but had not been validated as criteria for collective agreement or logical consistency. Appendix A.2 gives the rule and its treatment of neutral and missing responses. Participant review assessed acceptance of the generated summary separately [30].

The platform displayed a live map of voting patterns using principal component analysis (PCA) and k-means clustering. Participants could locate their highlighted position and inspect statements supported or opposed within their assigned group. Groups reflected recorded responses and were updated when responses changed (Figure 2(e)).

Researchers also prepared retrospective voting-pattern maps for the separate citizen workshop report. Those maps used different data processing and do not reconstruct the live participant display; Appendix A.3 contains the maps and their stability checks.

We inspected the pre-workshop code, interfaces and exports. The inspected code specified gpt-4o-transcribe for transcription and gpt-4o-mini for proposition extraction and summary generation (Appendix A.2). Logs did not establish every deployed setting, including whether optional automatic approval was active. We therefore do not assume it was used. Cards lacked source passages and previous wording, edit times and editor identities. A shared recorder label covered different speakers and workshop stages, while extraction treated each passage as one contribution and returned at most one claim. These gaps constrained the tracing analysis below. Appendix A.2 documents the processing rules, grouping calculations and retained data.

We developed the platform for real-world use in citizen workshops and assemblies. Beyond the workshops reported here, a civic group outside Switzerland continues to use it in another language for monthly in-person deliberative workshops involving more than 100 citizens per session.

The platform is open source and hosted on GitHub: https://github.com/Carbon-copy-PS/murmi.

3.5 Data collection and preparation

The study used shared boards from the human-moderated workflow, platform records from the AI-supported workflow and paper questionnaires about participants’ experiences. These sources captured different aspects of each workshop.

Discussion boards.

Researchers photographed the shared boards and transcribed notes into a dataset retaining room, topic, contribution type and grouping labels. The records preserve selected written contributions and their organisation, without an equivalent speech transcript or per-note voting dataset for the human-moderated workflow.

Platform records and summary reviews.

The platform retained machine-generated speech transcripts, cards, available approval and edit metadata, and proposition responses. These records let us examine how spoken or typed contributions entered the voting material. No project audio was retained for retranscription, and edit and response histories were incomplete.

The platform retained successive summaries, supporting proposition references, participants’ “good enough” or “not good enough” judgements and optional reasons, separately from the paper questionnaires. We compared these with finalised room-report snapshots. The retained response log and final-report aggregates differed, so we kept them distinct when reconstructing activity and support (Appendix A.1).

Participant questionnaires.

Participants completed paper questionnaire sections after each discussion and a final comparison after both formats. After each discussion, they answered eight questions from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Five assessed the output’s coverage of views, recognition of minority views, visibility of disagreement, traceability and acceptability as a fair summary. Three assessed opportunities to correct the output, the feeling of being heard and group control. The group-control item asked about participants’ experience and did not directly measure their decision-making authority. Questions referred to the physical board after the human-moderated workflow and the digital summary after the AI-supported workflow.

A ninth item asked about learning something new. An optional comment asked about missing or misrepresented views, disagreements or uncertainty. The supplement provides the full multilingual instrument.

After both discussions, participants compared output accuracy, the feeling of being heard, the handling of disagreement and future-workshop preferences. Scales ran from 1 (human much better) through 4 (about the same) to 7 (AI much better), with preference wording for future workshops. Two optional prompts asked about the most important difference and its effect, and what helped, hindered or should be changed in the platform.

Transcription, translation and linkage.

Two team members handled both transcription and translation of their assigned workshop materials. The team member responsible for the Zurich workshops was a native German speaker and transcribed the sticky notes and questionnaires, translating the German-language material into English. The second author, a native French speaker, was the team member responsible for the Lausanne workshops. She transcribed and documented the questionnaires, prepared the Lausanne board material, and translated the French questionnaire responses and discussion transcripts into English. The questionnaire transcriptions were organised into a structured response dataset, with scans retained as source material. Responses originally written in English were used directly. The resulting English questionnaire texts formed the corpus for the thematic analysis described below. The first author analysed these English texts and did not undertake translation. Preparing those responses was separate from the platform’s automatic transcription of the spoken discussion.

We linked questionnaire responses to demographic information using study codes where available. Linking questionnaire responses to app activity required a separate match, and we identified any uncertainty. We connected participants’ descriptions to their votes or summary-review responses only where the evidence supported that match.

Context from facilitation.

The workshop lead’s later reflections helped us understand the instructions, corrections, reporting work and differences between rooms. We distinguished these from participant comments and compared them with stored records where possible. They did not independently evaluate usability or workload.

3.6 Research analysis

Questionnaire ratings and written answers address participants’ experiences across formats (RQ 1). Transcripts, cards, response logs, summary versions and written review reasons support tracing how contributions and concerns entered the outputs of the AI-supported workflow (RQ 2); board records document the output of the human-moderated workflow. We describe the survey coding, illustrative excerpt interpretation and tracing procedures separately below.

3.6.1 Comparing participants’ ratings across formats

We averaged the eight representation-and-participation items when at least six were answered to form the study-specific overall representation-and-participation rating. Because this rating combines judgements about the output and participation, we give priority to the individual items when interpreting it. Complete-case internal consistency was \(\alpha=0.79\) after the human-moderated workflow (\(n=71\)) and \(\alpha=0.88\) after the AI-supported workflow (\(n=72\)) [9].

We calculated each participant’s AI-supported rating minus their human-moderated rating and averaged these differences across the 75 participants with paired overall ratings. Positive differences favoured the AI-supported workflow; negative differences favoured the human-moderated workflow. We applied the same calculation to each individual item using its available pairs. We report a 95% interval from resampling participants [12], sign-flip and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and the standardised paired effect \(d_z\). Benjamini–Hochberg and Holm adjustments addressed multiple comparisons across the eight individual items and, separately, the four direct comparisons [5, 16]. We compared future-workshop preference across rooms using a permutation Kruskal–Wallis test. Because the formats differed in sequence, topic and discussion structure, these comparisons do not isolate the effect of AI.

3.6.2 Qualitative analysis of questionnaire answers

Thematic analysis of survey answers.

The first author developed and reviewed the codebook, drawing both on the questionnaire responses and on prior concepts, and conducted the thematic analysis. Using this codebook, the first author grouped the English versions of 148 written answers from 67 respondents into descriptive thematic categories. The unit of coding was one answer to one question, with multiple categories allowed per answer. The corpus comprised 57 method comparisons, 53 platform comments, 20 comments after the human-moderated workflow and 18 after the AI-supported workflow. Blanks and missing-value placeholders were excluded; reports of no concern were retained.

We used six categories for method comparisons, eight for platform comments and six for post-discussion comments. An answer could acknowledge useful synthesis while also describing difficulties in following the discussion. The supplementary codebook provides the categories, coding rules and examples.

All reported survey category counts derive from the first author’s thematic coding. We also counted answers assigned to both a synthesis category and a discussion-related category within the same question, to examine whether these assessments occurred together in individual answers. This count used the existing category assignments; no new categories were introduced. Counts describe the submitted answers within each question; categories can overlap, and one respondent can contribute answers to several questions. They therefore cannot estimate the prevalence of an experience among workshop participants. Stable identifiers P1–P76 link questionnaire responses; uncertain links to app activity are identified separately.

Excerpt selection and interpretation.

The second author selected and checked the quotations. Excerpts were translated where needed by the team members responsible for transcription and translation and edited for readability, with interpretive paraphrases marked. Selected comments were checked against respondents’ recorded preferences.

The selected questionnaire excerpts cover all four rooms, different future preferences and positive, critical and mixed experiences. Post-discussion examples include a report of no missing content alongside concerns about representation. Appendix B.1 documents this illustrative selection.

3.6.3 Following contributions from discussion to report

We transcribed 140 board rows, retaining room, topic, contribution types and grouping labels. For the AI-supported workflow, the first author read all 143 transcript entries and 157 retained candidate cards chronologically, recording source, approval, duplication, meaning changes and workshop stage. A serious meaning change altered the actor, object or position enough to express a materially different claim. A stage error turned procedural speech into a policy proposition. Transcript-to-card matching relied on wording and timing because source links were absent from the stored records and no audio was retained under the consent protocol. The counts cover only clearly identifiable examples; they do not estimate error rates.

To follow correction episodes from discussion to cards and summaries, the first author compared passages across all four transcripts with card wording, creation times, edit flags, responses and summary references. Unreliable speaker identification precluded assigning survey codes to transcript speakers. Missing histories prevented complete reconstruction or linking a review judgement to a particular change (Appendix D). We examined exchanges about displayed groups alongside questionnaire comments, without reconstructing individual screen views or assignments.

To assess the evidence attached to the reported summary examples, we checked whether summary references resolved to published propositions, inspected the code handling them, and read contrasting linked-text examples alongside responses and comments. This distinguished a working reference from a proposition that substantively supported a summary claim (Appendix C.3).

3.6.4 Comparing votes and summary-review responses

Further checks covered 136 approved propositions, 45 summary items, 58 supporting links and four finalised room reports. The final aggregates contained 2,579 votes. The analysed event log contained 2,415 votes after excluding host activity. The incomplete timestamped log therefore supported different analyses from the final aggregates. Appendix B.3 gives their reconciliation and the coverage and timing calculations. We also examined whether later cards received fewer votes (Figure 9) and whether first-summary reviewers had been more active voters. The appendix also defines how response coverage was calculated.

The archive contained nine summary versions and 107 review responses. Seven versions were generated before the final room reports, with 105 review responses attached to them. The two later Zurich German versions accounted for the remaining two responses. Comparisons used anonymous identifiers. We paired judgements where identifiers matched and examined all ten written reasons. Appendix C.1 reports these records and the comparison between summary approval and support for cited propositions. Review responses lacked timestamps and reviewer sets changed across versions. We also compared each room’s last pre-report summary with the propositions and responses present when that report was finalised. Updated proposition responses retained only their latest value and timestamp.

4 Findings

We begin with questionnaire ratings and written responses about the physical boards and digital summaries and participants’ experience of contributing across the two workshop formats (RQ 1). We then use activity records and recorded exchanges to examine how participants and moderators worked with cards and summaries while discussion continued. Finally, we compare votes with summary-review responses and examine participants’ reasons for accepting or questioning the group summary (RQ 2).

4.1 Participants distinguished expressing views from discussing them together

Questionnaire responses distinguished registering a position from taking part in a shared conversation. Some participants valued one while criticising the other in the same response.

4.1.1 Ratings of the discussion outputs and the experience of participating

After each discussion, participants rated the resulting summary and their experience of taking part. The combined representation-and-participation rating showed no clear overall difference: means were 5.40 after the human-moderated workflow and 5.17 after the AI-supported workflow, with a paired difference of \(-0.23\) (95% CI \(-0.51\) to \(+0.04\), sign-flip \(p=0.104\)). These contrasts describe the workshop formats as experienced, rather than an isolated effect of AI.

Group-control ratings averaged 0.76 points lower after the AI-supported workflow (95% CI \(-1.20\) to \(-0.32\), Holm \(p=0.018\)). Ratings concerning accuracy, minority views, disagreement, traceability and acceptance of the summary showed no clear overall difference, while correction opportunities and the feeling of being heard also leaned towards the human-moderated workflow (Figure 5). The ratings describe perceived influence and experience; decision authority was not measured directly.

The separate final questionnaire asked participants to compare the formats directly. For output accuracy, 51.4% favoured the AI-supported workflow and 37.5% favoured the human-moderated workflow; for handling disagreement and less common views, the shares were 50.0% and 31.4%, respectively. Feeling heard favoured the human-moderated workflow: 49.3% preferred it on this criterion, compared with 27.4% preferring the AI-supported workflow. Only feeling heard showed a clear preference after Holm adjustment across the four direct comparisons (Holm \(p=0.015\)). The other comparisons describe tendencies in these workshops. Table 3 reports the means, response counts and shares rating the formats about the same, alongside contrasting participant comments.

The written responses help explain what participants valued about each format. Participants who favoured the AI-supported workflow described broader capture, easier expression and efficiency. Those who favoured the human-moderated workflow valued nuance, clarification and discussing together. 4.1.2 Voting and typing offered additional ways to contribute

P2 described voting as easier than confronting a speaker when disagreeing (Lausanne English). P69 valued being able to respond to every statement and described more limited opportunities to express a position in the human-moderated workflow (Zurich German, translated; see Table 3). These responses describe ways to register a view beyond taking a speaking turn.

Typing also helped some participants contribute. P29 described what helped as “to be able to write, to be heard even if shy” (Zurich English), while also reporting distraction and less listening. P70 valued posting an opinion immediately (Zurich German, paraphrased). Participants also valued recording: P45 described live transcription and the creation of arguments from participants’ ideas as helpful, and P46 said real-time capture kept thoughts from being forgotten (Zurich English, paraphrased). These are perceived benefits of contributing and recording. They do not establish complete capture or equal influence.

4.1.3 Participants valued the AI-generated outputs but wanted more discussion

Among the 57 written comparisons, 32 were coded for a human discussion or contextual-detail advantage, 23 for accuracy or detail concerns, and 20 for AI synthesis, structure or visibility. Five of those 20 answers also carried the human discussion or contextual-detail code. Some respondents therefore described both useful synthesis and an advantage of human discussion in the same answer. P24’s comparison illustrates how broader capture could coexist with less discussion:

“Less discussion + critical reflections came in the bigger AI moderated discussion. I preferred the AI-moderated output which captured more statements.” (P24, Zurich English, verbatim excerpt)

P24 favoured the human-moderated workflow for a future workshop (3/7), despite preferring the AI-supported output. Their comment describes less discussion in the AI-supported round, although both workflows began in small groups before sharing with the room. We interpret the comment as an account of how much discussion P24 experienced.

Several participants described the difficulty of contributing through the app while following the discussion:

“difficult to assess or vote and contribute to the debate at the same time.” (P1, Lausanne English)

P1 suggested a quiet review period; P65 reported distraction while writing and found Post-its more collaborative (Zurich German, paraphrased). Of 53 platform answers, 25 concerned synthesis or visualisation, 14 concerned divided attention or reduced exchange, and eight concerned insufficient time or pacing. Nine of the 25 synthesis answers also concerned divided attention or reduced exchange. These codes show which concerns appeared in the same answers. Cognitive overload was not measured.

Later cards generally received fewer votes and also left less time to respond before reporting (Appendix B.3, Figure 9). These voting patterns do not establish whether participants were listening at the same time.

The Zurich German comments and the workshop lead’s recollection raised a possible connection between the opening activity and participants’ attention. The workshop lead felt that asking people to enter statements first drew attention towards completing entries in the app and away from talking together. An early transcript passage notes that participants had already entered many points and invited a short spoken round. P58 similarly described less of a group result when everyone wrote their own opinion (paraphrased from German). These descriptions suggest competition between activities, while the transcript shows that spoken discussion continued. However, they do not isolate the effect of the opening instructions.

Participants also described what they wanted from the exchange. P48 wanted the tool embedded in social interaction and wrote, “I would have liked to hear from the others more, maybe switch tables etc.” (Zurich English, verbatim excerpt). Read alongside P24’s concern about critical reflection and P58’s description of individual writing, this suggests that opportunities to contribute did not always provide the shared discussion these participants wanted.

The workshop lead and one participant described different experiences of documentation and discussion. The workshop lead described less manual summary writing and more attention available for guiding the discussion, with moderator numbers unchanged between formats. P26 valued report generation while noting missed speech:

“the arguments are more individual, less collaborative. But very efficient for workshop host!” (P26, Zurich English)

P26 selected the midpoint for future-method preference. They saw the workflow as efficient for the host while expressing concerns about how it affected shared discussion. Neither their comment nor the workshop lead’s reflection measured effort saved.

P20’s and P67’s comments expressed contrasting expectations of deliberation and consensus (Table 6).

Participants’ assessments distinguished the physical board or digital summary from their experience of contributing to it (RQ 1). Some valued broader capture and the additional ways to express a position through voting or typing, while wanting more exchange with others. The ratings also showed lower perceived group control after the AI-supported workflow, and correction opportunities leaned towards the human-moderated workflow. Valuing the resulting board or summary did not necessarily mean feeling heard or able to influence it. These assessments concern participants’ experiences of the workshop arrangements; they do not establish whether their views changed.

4.2 Participants challenged meaning as discussion became cards and summaries

Voting continued after the first summary was generated in all four rooms, and three rooms produced a second version before reporting (Figure 6). In Zurich English and Lausanne French, additional propositions also entered between versions. Participants were still contributing and responding while the summary developed.

During this overlap, participants and moderators questioned wording, added statements and challenged what summaries included, emphasised or left out. They discussed both the meaning of individual cards and what the combined summary conveyed about the group. The following episodes trace what participants asked to preserve, what they and moderators did, and what appeared in the later record. Appendix B.2 presents participants’ concerns about selection in the human-moderated workflow.

4.2.1 Participants and moderators worked through corrections together

When participants questioned how a contribution had been captured, correcting the card became another task to work through during the discussion. In Lausanne English, an early exchange concerned whether the state had sufficient AI expertise to govern it. The machine transcript records a request to make a statement specifically about the state’s fluency, discussion of deleting the wrong statement and an objection that the text was almost the reverse of what had been said. It also records an instruction to let the app’s transcription run in the background and continue the discussion. In one example recalled by the workshop lead, the moderator revised a card about the government’s role after a participant explained that its wording did not capture their intended meaning. The lead also recalled a participant entering another card. The correction illustrates how a participant could shape the wording presented for voting through an exchange with the moderator.

An approved card from this interval described the state’s need for capability to govern AI. It was marked edited, received seven responses and was cited by two recommendations in the final summary. Its earlier wording and edit time were not retained, so the stored record cannot establish which action produced this version or whether the participant accepted the revised wording. Appendix B.3 reports the wider edit counts and capture problems.

Direct entry offered another way to contribute, but one participant in Zurich German described uncertainty about how to use it:

“I find the report good and interesting, but I do not understand how it came about, so I am a little mistrustful. No one explained to me how to create an ‘opinion card’ that the whole group could then ‘vote’ on.” (P67, translated)

P67 also selected 6 on the final future-preference scale, favouring the AI-supported workflow. This respondent’s favourable preference accompanied uncertainty about the process; it does not establish how other participants understood the platform.

4.2.2 Tracing concerns through cards and successive summaries

The following cases show what was preserved and what changed as discussion became voting cards and summaries. We matched spoken comments to cards using wording and timing because the records did not directly link them.

Lausanne English: a workload concern retained across summaries.

In Lausanne English, the discussion moved from preparing workers to use AI to protecting them from growing work pressures. Someone described “a real risk of mental overload and burnout” and added, “I start to feel it myself.” Reducing working hours was then suggested as a possible response to more intense work. These ideas became separate voting cards about overload, work intensity and shorter hours. The votes supported the two descriptions of the problem more widely than the proposal to reduce hours, which also received neutral responses.

Both summary versions carried the issue forward as an open question: “How can we assess the impact of AI integration on worker mental health and workload?” Neither presented shorter hours as an agreed recommendation. Here, the shared summary retained the concern while preserving a distinction between recognising a problem and agreeing on what to do about it. Table 13 provides the detailed trace.

Lausanne French: what the shorter statement left out.

When the first Lausanne French summary described broad agreement on integrating AI learning, someone objected: “We have resolved absolutely nothing.” Later, when the group was asked what was missing, someone said school must reinvent itself but questioned “where” and “how”, and said the summary left out the emotion in the discussion. A voting card created in the same minute read “School must reinvent itself”. It kept the call for change but left out the uncertainty about what that change should mean. These excerpts are translated from French.

The ten votes recorded for this statement were all in favour, yet written feedback continued to question the summary: “Same comment as before. Too much flattening of arguments and ideas.” Most of the summary’s wording stayed the same. People had another chance to explain what was missing and vote, but agreement with a short statement did not show that the summary captured what mattered to them. We cannot tell whether the repeated criticism referred to this particular card. Appendix D.2 gives the full sequence, including the timing and limits of the records.

The records do not establish whether these exchanges explain changes in approval. Appendix C.3 provides a further example from Zurich English.

Participants and moderators worked with the cards and summaries by clarifying wording, adding contributions and discussing what a card or summary missed (RQ 2). Their questions concerned the meaning of individual contributions and whether the summary preserved uncertainty and unresolved differences. The English summary kept a workload concern open without settling on a solution; the French card kept a call for change while leaving out uncertainty about its direction. The recalled moderator correction shows one way a participant influenced a card, while the continuing French objections show that further discussion and voting did not necessarily settle concerns about the summary.

We also checked the propositions cited by the summaries. In Zurich English, eight second-version summary items cited the same proposition about workforce replacement, including items about training and health-care ethics. Even when a link opened an existing proposition, that proposition could be unrelated to the summary claim. Appendix C.3 gives the source-link audit and a contrasting example with relevant references.

4.3 Supporting statements did not necessarily mean accepting the summary

Participants could mark a summary “good enough” or “not good enough” and explain their response. Accepting a group summary could differ from endorsing its recommendations. Two reviews of the second Lausanne English summary illustrate the distinction:

“I disagree with the recommendations, but it’s OK with what the group thinks” (Accepted)

“Missing texture, details and color. Some issues dropped. Some misunderstood.” (Rejected)

The first response distinguishes personal agreement from accepting a description of the group’s views. The second questions what that description preserves. Neither response can be linked to a particular edit. Appendix C.1 retains all written review reasons and Appendix C.2 examines agreement with cited propositions alongside summary-review responses.

Linking votes with summary-review responses also showed support for propositions alongside rejection of a summary. Of the 17 responses rejecting a first summary, eleven were linked to votes supporting at least three quarters of the cited propositions answered. The calculation uses the latest retained responses; votes at the time of review could not be reconstructed. It also depends on which propositions were cited, and those links could be incomplete or unrelated to the summary claims. It does not explain the reasons for each rejection (Appendix C.2).

The five written reasons accompanying first-version rejections concerned missing content, lost nuance, and summaries that made the discussion seem more settled than it was. A separate survey response from P5 described a perceived mismatch in emphasis:

“We had a long discussion about if the government was able to take on that role and it’s appearing as a detail in the report (hierarchy is missing).” (P5, Lausanne English)

In Lausanne French’s discussion of AI in education, P16 and P17 likewise reported that the role or purpose of schooling remained unclear or underrepresented in the final output. These questionnaire responses concern the same topic as the review episodes, but the records do not identify these respondents as the speakers or reviewers in those sequences or connect their answers to a particular edit.

Concerns about emphasis also extended across formats. P70 felt that human presentations conveyed what particularly mattered to each presenter, while the AI result appeared smoothed and optimised (Zurich German, paraphrased). P17 reported that ideas had not been prioritised in the human-moderated workflow either (Lausanne French, paraphrased). These comments distinguish including an issue from preserving its perceived importance; they do not establish an agreed weighting of the discussion’s concerns.

Review responses across summary versions.

Three rooms reviewed a second summary before reporting (Figure 7). Lausanne French’s approval fell from 75% (9 of 12 review responses) to 69% (9 of 13): nine responses approved each version, but the responses did not all come from the same accounts or devices. Of ten reviews paired across versions using anonymous identifiers, one changed from approval to rejection and none changed towards approval. Review ended below the 80% target because the 45-minute time limit left no time for another iteration. Lausanne English rose from 40% (2/5) to 86% (6/7). Of its five paired reviews, two changed to approval and none changed away. Approval also rose in Zurich English, while Zurich German completed review at 80% with the first version. These snapshots combine changes in responses with differences in who submitted a review. They do not isolate effects of particular revisions. Appendix C.1 reports all linked judgements.

The two complete Zurich English summaries are compared in Appendix C.3.

In reviewing summaries, participants judged how the group was represented as well as whether they agreed with particular statements. Written reasons questioned missing concerns, their relative emphasis and the impression that unresolved issues had been settled. Acceptance could accompany disagreement with recommendations, while support for cited propositions could accompany rejection of the summary. Overall approval and changes between versions do not establish that a particular concern was resolved.

5 Discussion

Participants valued AI support for some aspects of the workshops and wanted more shared discussion in others. Around half favoured the AI-supported workflow for output accuracy and handling disagreement or less common views. Participants more often favoured the human-moderated workflow for feeling heard. Future-workshop preferences were mixed: 33% favoured the AI-supported workflow, 40% the human-moderated workflow and 26% rated them alike (Table 3). These assessments concern the workshop arrangements as a whole and cannot be attributed to AI alone.

Cards and summaries sometimes became part of the discussion itself. A card could offer another way to express a position while losing a qualification that mattered to its speaker. A summary could be accepted as a fair representation of the group despite personal disagreement with its recommendations. Participants drew on their experience of the conversation to question these interpretations.

We examine this relationship through dynamic participatory sensemaking: discussion shapes cards and summaries, and participants’ responses to them can prompt clarification, further contributions and revision. Participation depends on whether people can influence those interpretations, including how their views appear alongside others’. In the correction recalled from Lausanne English, a participant helped shape a card’s wording. In Lausanne French, concerns persisted through further discussion and voting. These episodes suggest that participants need to recognise their meaning in the cards and summaries, have time to consider them together and be able to follow what happens to requests for change. Whether this process improves mutual understanding or agreement remains to be studied.

5.1 Preserving people’s meaning before drawing views together

For someone who has just explained why an issue matters to them, seeing their words return on a voting card can invite a difficult question: is this still what I meant? It also gives others another way to engage with the idea. Some participants valued being able to register disagreement without confronting the speaker, and direct entry offered a way to express a view when it was difficult to get a speaking turn (Section 4.1). A card could give an idea more attention than it received in the spoken exchange, provided people could still recognise the meaning behind its wording.

The cases suggest that participants need different things from voting cards and discussion summaries. A voting card needs to preserve an individual’s intended meaning; a discussion summary needs to represent the group’s views. The Lausanne French card about schooling omitted the speaker’s uncertainty about what change should mean (Section 4.2.2), while a reviewer in Lausanne English accepted a summary despite disagreeing with its recommendations (Section 4.3). Review needs to allow participants to question both a card’s wording and the account of the group’s discussion.

We therefore argue for keeping argument extraction close to what people say. A voting card should preserve the speaker’s reasons, conditions and uncertainty, so that others respond to the view they intended to express. Where a short card cannot carry this context, an accompanying explanation could make it available before voting. The discussion summary can then draw views together, showing shared concerns alongside differences and unresolved questions. Giving each step a clearer purpose could help people first feel understood in their own terms, then consider how their views belong in a summary they share with others.

For that possibility to become part of everyday workshop practice, people need ways to explain what a card misses, attach a reason or bring a disputed statement back to the room. Voting can open these conversations, but a response count cannot tell us whether someone has considered an idea or understood what its author meant [7, 34]. Our records likewise do not establish whether people became more open-minded, changed their views or understood one another better.

5.2 Dynamic participatory sensemaking redistributes work and capability

Dynamic participatory sensemaking brings some of the work of interpreting a discussion into the exchange between participants and moderators. Choices about selection and emphasis that researchers would make during later reporting became open to participants while they were still in the room. Participants could explain what a card missed and question how a summary brought their views together. Researchers retained responsibility for interpreting the material and preparing the findings. Supporting this process therefore requires attention to who can question an interpretation, who helps work through it and who carries a requested change forward.

This work extended across discussion, voting cards and summaries. In Lausanne English, participants and moderators clarified an intended contribution and coordinated a card correction. Its wording mattered to the speaker, to people voting on it and to those preparing the summary. The coordination involved in opening reporting to participant review is a form of articulation work [28, 31]. Missing edit history limits how fully we can reconstruct its outcome.

Review also competed for attention: 14 of 53 platform comments mentioned divided attention, and eight mentioned insufficient time. The workshop lead described less manual writing and more attention for facilitation, with unchanged staffing and no measured time saving. Participants checked meaning, moderators coordinated review and researchers interpreted incomplete records. This effort deserves recognition alongside the finished report [28, 31].

These experiences suggest testing a different workshop rhythm: discussion, time to read and correct cards, voting, and a return to conversation before summary review. Participants could focus on each activity and return to earlier contributions when needed. Future work should compare this phased format with overlapping discussion and voting under comparable conditions, examining whether participants feel heard, can follow through on corrections and prefer the experience.

These opportunities need to be accessible beyond those already active in the app. Identifiers with a first-summary review response had voted on a larger share of the available cards before that summary was generated. P67 favoured the AI-supported workflow but said no one had explained how to create a card. A short practice activity, spoken correction requests and moderator invitations to less-active participants could help people take part. Future studies should examine who needs help, who raises corrections and whose concerns reach later versions. Our study did not establish whether particular demographic groups were disadvantaged. These proposals need evaluation alongside the phased format.

Listening, clarifying misunderstandings and working through disagreements still call for human skill. Supporting dynamic participatory sensemaking therefore means making room for this work alongside voting and summary production.

5.3 From richer records to accountable public reporting

Reading transcripts, cards, votes and summaries together helped us see how contributions were represented and where concerns remained unresolved. Readers encountering only the final report would have less access to these exchanges. We did not study how policymakers or other readers used the reports. Future work should examine whether showing the sources behind recommendations, alongside participant feedback and revision histories, helps readers understand how a report was produced and what remained unresolved. This would also require deciding which material participants are comfortable sharing beyond the workshop.

Missing source passages and revision histories made it difficult to establish what supported a summary claim. Final reports could also combine a reviewed summary with later changes to propositions or responses. A polished summary backed by counts may appear more authoritative than these records warrant. Readers need access to the relevant sources and the exact version participants reviewed. Accepting a proposition, accepting a summary and authorising a public report remain different judgements [25].

Participants should also be able to follow what happened to a concern they raised. Summary claims could link to source cards and responses, with objections linked to revisions or marked unresolved. After an edit, the person who raised the concern should be asked whether the new wording does it justice. Reports should distinguish how many people reviewed a summary from how many approved it, and show whether later changes were reviewed. Such a record could also give citizens a basis for asking how public institutions responded to their recommendations.

These opportunities to question the record still relied on how the moderator responded. Selection and wording could steer what became available for a vote, even when the voting itself was transparent. Participants could follow edits on screen, but did not have a complete record of what had been changed or left out. In wider use, participants should be able to review these decisions and raise concerns with someone other than the moderator. The software would also need to prevent duplicate accounts and moderator voting, and limit spam, without making participation unnecessarily difficult. Our study did not establish that moderator review prevented bias or deliberate manipulation.

Making the record easier to inspect also needs to respect the privacy commitments described in Section 3.3. A source passage that explains a summary may contain context that identifies its speaker. Participant access to evidence within a workshop and public access to a report therefore need separate decisions about what can be shown and to whom. Future designs should evaluate whether restricted source access and approved excerpts provide enough context to question a claim while limiting exposure. Permission to publish should concern the particular report and supporting material to be released; accepting a summary during a workshop does not settle that decision.

5.4 Extending deliberation across places, times and languages

A shared record could help people continue a discussion remotely, return later with further thoughts or encounter contributions from other groups in their preferred language. They could revisit one another’s reasons, add what was missing and carry a face-to-face conversation beyond the meeting. Our workshops used German, English and French in separate groups and did not test these forms of exchange. Participants would need ways to check translated contributions and correct wording that no longer conveyed their meaning. Remote, asynchronous and cross-language participation remain directions for future study.

A parliamentary debate offers one example of what this could look like. In a multilingual setting such as Switzerland, citizens could follow contributions in their preferred language, respond to statements drawn from the debate and add reasons or questions from elsewhere. Some could participate as the debate unfolds; others could return to the material later. Representatives could consider these contributions during or after the debate. Such a process would need ways to check meaning across translations and make clear how citizens’ contributions enter the exchange.

Following discussions over time could show whether participants can get cards and summaries revised, respond to one another’s reasons and keep unresolved concerns in view. The aim is for deliberation to continue beyond one meeting, while keeping later recommendations connected to the concerns, disagreements and hopes people expressed.

6 Limitations

The study had to fit the practical needs of four workshops held as part of a public consultation. Citizens first took part in a human-moderated workflow to become familiar with the workshop before taking part in the AI-supported workflow. Participants’ experience in the first round may have influenced how they approached the second, making the workflows difficult to compare independently. Topics, languages, group sizes and facilitation also varied across rooms, so the findings reflect how these elements worked together in this setting. Demographic information was available for only some participants, which further limits our understanding of how their backgrounds may have shaped their experiences.

To understand how participants’ concerns reached the summaries, we read the transcripts alongside cards, responses and successive versions of the text. This made some changes visible, but we had no retained audio to return to, and source links and parts of the edit history were missing. Connections based on wording and timing therefore leave some uncertainty about how a change came about. The records also do not establish whether participants felt that the revision addressed their concern.

Participants’ ratings and comments help explain what they valued and what made the workshops difficult, though they offer less insight into how their views developed or how well they came to understand one another. Since comments were optional and sometimes translated, the responses presented here also depend on who chose to respond and how we interpreted their words. The workshop lead’s later reflections helped us understand the demands of facilitation, but further work would be needed to establish how the platform changed the effort required of participants, moderators and researchers.

Working closely with the platform as its designers and facilitators gave us insight into its use, while also making our own role part of the setting we studied. Citizens and facilitators may find different ways of working with it as they gain experience or use it for other purposes. Following those adaptations over time, along with what happens to reports after a workshop, would help establish how AI support fits into a deliberative process that citizens themselves can shape.

7 Conclusion

We developed Murmi to explore dynamic participatory sensemaking through live discussion, voting and summary review. Across four language-group workshops at two Swiss events, questionnaire responses from 76 participants more often favoured the AI-supported workflow for output accuracy, while the human-moderated workflow was more often favoured for feeling heard. These assessments reflect the workshop arrangements as a whole and cannot be attributed to AI alone.

Participants valued additional ways to express their views, but contributing through voting and typing did not always provide the exchange they wanted with others. Their questions about cards and summaries also showed why representation belongs within that exchange: wording could retain a topic while losing its uncertainty, and support for individual statements did not necessarily mean accepting how the discussion was summarised. Reviewing these materials gave participants opportunities to explain what they meant and question how contributions were brought together, although the records did not consistently establish whether their concerns were resolved.

In these workshops, dynamic participatory sensemaking involved participants returning to cards and summaries to consider how their views were represented. Supporting it requires time to consider contributions together, ways to challenge wording and emphasis, and follow-through on requests for revision. Participants and moderators help determine what the discussion means and what remains unsettled. Designs should give participants a continuing say in how their contributions become shared summaries and public recommendations.

Ethical Considerations and Adverse Impacts

The ETH Zurich Ethics Commission approved the protocol (project 26 ETHICS-116) and amendment before fieldwork. Section 3.3 describes consent, data protection and the opportunities participants had to challenge generated cards and summaries. These safeguards leave questions that matter beyond the workshops: who can influence what is included, whether an objection receives a meaningful response, and what participants have actually authorised when they accept a summary. We do not treat support for propositions or acceptance of a summary as evidence of consensus or permission to publish a report on the group’s behalf. Wider use would also require access controls that prevent duplicate or unauthorised participation without unnecessarily collecting personal information or linking names to votes. For example, organisers could issue each attendee a unique random access code, with controlled replacement, to limit duplicate entries without storing names alongside votes. These controls and their effectiveness against deliberate manipulation remain to be tested.

A Pol.is-inspired pipeline [29] projected voting patterns into two dimensions with PCA. K-means compared two to four groups where possible and selected the highest silhouette score. Support rates excluded neutral responses, whereas coverage included them and used the app-record roster. Equation 1 gives the project-specific rule for suggesting combinations of propositions. The implementation called split support “conflict”. The thresholds supplied guidance to the model. They did not validate collective agreement or the generated summary.

Joint-support rule.

Two propositions can each attract majority support from different coalitions, so approval of each separately does not establish collective backing of their combination [23]. Checking joint and split support gives the summary model evidence about where these coalitions overlap and which disagreements a combined recommendation could obscure. Before suggesting that the model combine them, the platform required both substantial joint support (supporting both) and limited split support (supporting one while opposing the other). For propositions \(j\) and \(k\), let \(B_{jk}\) and \(F_{jk}\) count these two patterns, respectively, and \(S_{jk}\) count all identifiers with non-neutral responses to both. A pair was flagged only if at least two records gave non-neutral responses to both, at least 50% supported both and at most 30% split their support: \[ \text{flag for combination}_{jk}= \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } S_{jk}\geq 2,\ B_{jk}\geq 0.5S_{jk},\ F_{jk}\leq 0.3S_{jk},\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \tag{1}\] For example, a pair with 60% joint support and 20% split support would qualify, whereas one with the same joint support but 40% split support would not. Records opposing both counted in \(S_{jk}\), but neither in \(B_{jk}\) nor \(F_{jk}\). Neutral or missing responses to either proposition were excluded. We chose these thresholds as project-specific heuristics. They had not been validated as criteria for collective agreement or tests of logical consistency. This rule guided the prompt without guaranteeing that the generated summary followed it or was acceptable to participants. Participant review assessed acceptance separately, consistent with proposals to return generated summaries to participants for checking [30].

B.3 Speech capture, card sources and response coverage

Across the four rooms, the archive contained 143 transcript entries and 157 candidate cards: 117 from speech, 37 entered directly, and three generated to highlight tensions. Moderators approved 136 cards, including 96 created from speech. The conservative audit identified two clear reversals, ten duplicate or near-duplicate cards and 19 candidates drawn from instructions, review, report narration or talk outside the policy discussion. Categories overlap. In Lausanne English, a claim about the state’s expertise was represented as a limitation in AI’s ability to educate workers. Later, a suggestion to begin with an imperfect law became a rejection of using an imperfect AI system. These examples show changes to what people were asked to vote on. They do not estimate the rate of transcription errors.

We ordered propositions by creation time within each room and divided them into four nearly equal groups. Coverage was the share of non-host identifiers in the timestamped log responding to a proposition. Support, neutrality and opposition used all recorded responses, including neutral responses, as their denominator. We also measured time from card creation to the final report. Card order and available response time were therefore intertwined. A further test accounting for room compared voting activity between first-summary reviewers and other identifiers. Updated responses retained only their latest value and timestamp.

The datasets are reconciled in Appendix A.1. Figure 9 shows response coverage and recorded choices across the card sequence. Card-order correlations with response coverage were \(\rho=-0.84\) in Zurich English, \(-0.92\) in Lausanne French and \(-0.56\) in Zurich German (adjusted \(p\leq0.00046\)), and \(-0.26\) in Lausanne English (adjusted \(p=0.119\)). Later cards also had less available time, so these associations cannot identify why coverage declined. We found no corresponding change in support or opposition over the sequence.

Across the 157 retained candidate cards, four were marked edited: two in Zurich German and one in each English-language room. All four were approved and received responses. The flag identifies cards whose wording changed, but does not establish the number of edits, whether voting preceded an edit or whether a correction resolved a concern.

An instruction in Zurich German to analyse the data first became an approved proposition and received 23 responses. Moderator approval therefore did not establish that every card represented a participant’s policy view.

Direct entry provided another input route. In Zurich German, 26 of 38 approved cards were entered directly and twelve came from audio. Their topics included age limits, basic skills, privacy, teacher training, assessment, equal access and human skills. People used direct entry for these topics, although the records leave open whether an entry repaired an omission or changed the later summary. The survey comments separately report a cut sentence that nearly reversed meaning (P29, Zurich English) and a request for two microphones (P2, Lausanne English). The decision to discard audio reduced privacy risks and limited later checks to the retained text.

Recorded voting activity extended across the AI-supported workflow. Among non-host identifiers with card responses, the median number of responses per record ranged from 18.5 to 36 across rooms, and the median interval between first and last recorded responses ranged from 5.5 to 23.0 minutes. Between 46% and 75% of consecutive responses were submitted within ten seconds of each other. These measures describe recorded voting activity. They cannot show whether participants were listening or inspecting a summary at the same time.

Among the 82 identifiers in the voting-log analysis, 61 had a recorded response to the first summary and 21 did not. Using retained votes timestamped before the first summary was generated in each room, median coverage of the propositions available by that point was 89.5% among those with a review response and 76.3% among those without one (Holm \(p=0.047\)). This difference does not establish exclusion or explain why a summary judgement was submitted. The late Lausanne French card in Section 4.2.2 was absent from summary citations, but the records do not establish that response coverage explains its absence.

C.2 Votes and summary-review responses

To compare agreement with source propositions and acceptance of their summary, we calculated support for each identifier with a summary judgement. Let \(J_{iv}\) contain the unique propositions cited by summary version \(v\) that non-host account or device identifier \(i\) answered, and let \(a_{ij}=1\) for agreement or strong agreement and \(0\) for any other recorded response. Its support share was \[ s_{iv}=\frac{1}{\lvert J_{iv}\rvert}\sum_{j\in J_{iv}}a_{ij}, \qquad \lvert J_{iv}\rvert>0. \tag{2}\] Each cited proposition counted once, even if several summary items cited it. Unlike the responses used in the pairwise rule in Equation 1, this measure included explicit neutral responses in the denominator. Missing responses were excluded, as were records with no answered cited propositions. We used the latest retained responses and pooled the first summary from each room, giving each included identifier equal weight within the approving and rejecting sets.

The 44 approving records supported an average of 77.9% of their answered cited propositions. The 17 rejecting records supported 76.1%. The difference was 1.8 percentage points (room-stratified permutation \(p=0.683\)). Eleven of the 17 rejecting records supported at least three quarters of their answered cited propositions. Reviewers could support many cited propositions and still reject the summary. These results do not establish statistical equivalence between the groups or identify why each record rejected the summary. The archive does not reconstruct votes at the moment of review, and a working citation need not substantively support a summary claim.
