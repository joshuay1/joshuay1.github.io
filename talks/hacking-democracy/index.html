Collective decisions

What can democracy look like?

Where do you already make decisions together?

Shared budgets · Workplace decisions · Citizens’ assemblies · Referendums

Begin with a working idea of democracy: people having a meaningful say in decisions that affect them. These everyday and civic examples invite questions about participation, power and shared decisions. A group choosing dinner is not a complete democratic political system; it lets us examine the same design questions on a small scale.

Collective decisions range from choosing dinner with friends and managing a shared-house budget to elections, citizens’ assemblies and public funding. Ask which settings people recognize. The examples are invitations to notice processes they already know.

Everyday coordination is not identical to public democracy: rights, authority and accountability differ. But these settings let us examine how a group shares information, deals with disagreement and chooses what to do. Use the range to widen possible project contexts.

Invite two quick examples from the room: where have you made a decision with other people this week? Start with dinner or a favourite song, then move to public decisions. Eurovision and dinner illustrate aggregation and coordination, not equivalence to political democracy. Avoid explaining current Eurovision rules here. The Swiss image is a conceptual illustration, not a geographically precise map.

Collective intelligence

How groups use what people know

A group’s knowledge is distributed. The process determines what becomes visible and usable.

Different knowledge

Invite experiences that others may not have.

→

Independent views

Collect initial views before people follow the crowd.

→

Structured exchange

Compare reasons, preserve disagreement and revise.

Agreement, accuracy and democratic legitimacy are different goals.

Collective intelligence asks how a group uses knowledge that no one person has alone. Democratic theory also asks who gets a say and who has the right to decide.

Imagine a neighbourhood deciding how to use a shared space. Different people know different needs. Discussion lets them compare those needs and consider changes to the proposals. A decision rule then determines the outcome.

Landemore argues that including different experiences and perspectives can improve public judgment. That is a reason to pay attention to participation and discussion, not a guarantee that every group will make a better decision. Equality, legitimate authority and the ability to challenge a result also matter.

These are design questions, not universal guarantees. Lorenz et al. (2011) show that social influence can reduce diversity in estimation tasks; Woolley et al. (2010) associate group performance with social sensitivity and turn-taking. See the reading list. Discussion may also reveal information unavailable in isolated judgments.

Research directions

Four research directions

Computational social choice

How should we count votes, share a budget or select representatives?

Deliberative democracy

How can discussion include different views and inform public decisions?

Human–AI interaction

How do people use, check and correct help from AI?

Complexity science & collective intelligence

How do interactions and networks shape what groups learn and decide?

These are overlapping fields, not a sequence of progress. The same budgeting tool can raise a social-choice question about allocation, a design question about understanding, and an ethics question about who is included.

Keep the field names because students can use them to find courses, researchers and papers. The questions underneath explain what each field studies in ordinary language.

Current work includes AI-assisted discussion, allocation rules with real-world constraints and ways to evaluate models of human behavior. The reading guide includes primary papers and explains what each finding does and does not show. Students can choose any of these research directions.

Computational social choice studies the properties of formal decision rules. Complexity science and collective intelligence study how group-level learning and behavior emerge from interactions, networks and feedback. Computational social science offers methods used across these directions; it is not presented as another kind of voting-rule design.

Democracy as an open-source project

Rules are inherited, adapted and changed along different paths.

United Kingdom

Parliamentary oversight

Parliamentary institutions influenced the US framers. The Parliament Acts of 1911 and 1949 later limited the Lords’ ability to block certain bills.

United States · 1787

Checks & balances

British and state precedents informed a federal constitution with a president and Congress. Its two-chamber federal model then influenced Switzerland and Australia.

Switzerland · 1848

Direct democracy

A federal parliament drawing on the US model. Later changes: optional law referendum (1874), initiative for partial constitutional revision (1891), and the first proportional National Council election (1919).

Australia · 1901

Compulsory voting

British parliamentary government combined with a US-inspired federal Senate. Later changes: House preferential voting (1918), compulsory federal voting (1924), and the first proportional Senate election (1949).

Selected influences and reforms · US Senate · Swiss Parliament · AEC

Democracy as an open-source project is a useful analogy: institutional rules can be inspected, borrowed and revised. The branching structure follows Motivation 1 in Self-Introduction.pptx. Follow the British line into the US branch, then the US branch into Switzerland and Australia. Australia also drew directly on British parliamentary government, as its entry label explains.

The short country labels pick out one feature of each branch, not an exclusive national invention. Parliamentary oversight: scrutinize government and require public explanations. Checks and balances: divide authority so each branch can constrain the others. Direct democracy: let citizens challenge laws and propose constitutional changes. Compulsory voting: address low participation; the 1924 reform aimed to increase turnout. These are design purposes, not guarantees that a system achieves them.

Different paths do not imply inevitable progress or equal access to political power. These systems excluded many people; Swiss women gained federal voting rights only in 1971. Kultur Komitee is a residents’ budget assembly in Winterthur. Participants consider art and cultural proposals, discuss them, assess them and decide how the available funding should be allocated.

Irish citizens’ assemblies use a selection process intended to reflect the public, evidence sessions and facilitated discussion before recommendations. Recommendations require political consideration and a response; they are not automatically binding decisions.

Civic technology can help people find and question evidence, record unresolved questions or follow the response to recommendations. Selection is also a research problem: Flanigan et al. study fairness of selection probabilities subject to demographic quotas.

For students, an evidence pack can be a sufficient project. Pick one question that an assembly would examine, link competing claims to their sources and test whether a reader can explain the remaining disagreement.

Start with this decision before introducing theory or history. Nine people rank a garden, a music event and a workshop. These are invented teaching ballots. Ask the audience which project should win.

With plurality, count only first choices: garden receives four, music three and workshop two. Ask what changes if second choices count. Switch to Borda: first gets two points, second one and third zero. Music wins with twelve points, ahead of garden on eight and workshop on seven.

A readable receipt can support a question about the allocation. It is not by itself a complete fairness or security audit. Research on digital voting interfaces asks how design affects perceived fairness, legitimacy and understanding.

Accountability continues after allocation. Decidim’s accountability component links results to implementation progress. Another small project could connect an approved proposal to its responsible body, milestones and evidence of delivery.

End with possibility: democratic institutions and everyday collective processes can change. You do not need to redesign the whole system to contribute. Start from curiosity, learn with others and make something worth trying. The direction is theirs to discover; an uncomplicated idea can make a meaningful contribution.

Choose a project or process you want to understand. Find out how it works, who is involved and what is already known. Investigate the context before committing to a solution.

Talk to the people involved, read relevant research and explore previous attempts. Ask what you still need to learn and which assumptions you should check.

Use that research to consider different possibilities and decide what you want to build. The projects in this talk are examples to learn from. Your direction can differ from theirs.

Democracy as an open-source project: historical sources

Institutional borrowing and later reforms

Adapted from Joshua’s Motivation 1, slide 3 of Self-Introduction.pptx. The diagram shows selected institutional influences. “Open source” is an analogy for studying and adapting rules; it does not imply equal access to political power or automatic improvement.

The 1787 constitutional compromise combined a House apportioned by population with two senators per state. US Senate

The 1848 Swiss Constitution established a bicameral federal parliament, using the United States as a model. Swiss Parliament: 1848

Australia combines parliamentary government responsible to the elected house, drawn from Westminster, with a federal Senate inspired by the United States. Australian Parliament

The Australian Constitution took effect with federation on 1 January 1901. Australian Constitution

The Parliament Acts of 1911 and 1949 permit certain public bills to pass without Lords consent. The 1949 Act reduced the delay for eligible non-money bills from two years to one. Money bills and other categories have different provisions; the Lords did not lose every veto. UK Parliament

The optional federal legislative referendum dates to 1874. The mandatory constitutional referendum already existed in 1848. Swiss Parliament: referendums

The federal popular initiative for partial constitutional revision dates to 1891. The initiative for total revision predates this, going back to 1848. Swiss Parliament: initiatives

Preferential voting for the House of Representatives was introduced in 1918; compulsory voting at federal elections in 1924. Senate proportional representation was enacted in 1948 and first used in 1949, not at federation in 1901. Australian Electoral Commission

Political participation was historically restricted. Swiss women gained federal voting rights in 1971. Swiss Federal Archives

The US framers drew on British institutions and state constitutions. The connection represents selected institutional precedents, not a literal fork of modern Westminster government. US Senate: British and state precedents

Proportional election of the National Council was approved in 1918 and first used in 1919. Swiss Parliament: proportional National Council elections

Citizens’ assemblies and consultations

Process. Participants examine evidence, discuss alternatives and produce recommendations or consultation outputs.

A concrete difficulty. An important concern can disappear when discussions are summarized.

Possible prototype. A source-linked correction or unresolved-question record.

Evaluation. Compare the account with original contributions; count unsupported claims and missing requirements.

Participatory budgeting

Process. Develop proposals, assess feasibility and allocate a limited budget using a defined procedure.

A concrete difficulty. A proposer needs to meet requirements; a participant needs to understand how the allocation was produced.

Possible prototype. A feasibility checklist, comparison of rules, or readable allocation receipt.

Evaluation. Hold ballots and costs fixed for a rule comparison; ask a participant to explain an allocation.

Research directions and selected papers

These fields overlap. The selection is a reading guide, not a chronology or an exhaustive map. Recent topics include AI-mediated deliberation, allocation with feasibility constraints and agents grounded in human self-reports.

Hélène Landemore: inclusive deliberation and cognitive diversity provides a political-theory starting point.

Computational social choice

Voting rules, proportional allocation and fair selection of representatives.

Proportional Participatory Budgeting with Additive Utilities

Peters, Pierczyński & Skowron · 2021 · NeurIPS
Peer-reviewed mathematical paper

Develops proportionality axioms and allocation rules for projects with different costs and additive utilities; a foundation for the Method of Equal Shares.

Limit. A guarantee depends on its preference model and precise axiom. It does not make every outcome fair under every definition.

Fair algorithms for selecting citizens’ assemblies

Flanigan, Gölz, Gupta, Hennig & Procaccia · 2021 · Nature
Peer-reviewed algorithms and deployment study

Optimizes selection probabilities among volunteers while meeting demographic quotas; evaluates fairness on data from ten assemblies.

Limit. Fairness within the volunteer pool does not solve unequal willingness or ability to volunteer.

A General Theory of Proportionality with Additive Utilities

Skowron · 2026 · arXiv:2602.08504
Preprint · February 2026

Proposes proportional rules for scored preferences under general feasibility constraints, and proportional rankings.

Limit. A theoretical proposal, not a deployment evaluation. Constraints and the definition of proportionality must be specified.

Generative Social Choice: The Next Generation

Boehmer, Fish & Procaccia · 2025 · ICML

Selects statements representing different groups under a text-length budget. Guarantees depend on formal preferences and query assumptions; a fluent summary alone does not establish fair representation.

Deliberative democracy

Inclusion, reason-giving, public judgment and legitimate authority.

Evidence for a Collective Intelligence Factor in the Performance of Human Groups

Woolley et al. · 2010 · Science
Peer-reviewed empirical study

Two studies with 699 people found a shared factor in small groups’ performance across tasks, associated with social sensitivity and conversational turn-taking.

Limit. These associations do not show that equal speaking time causes better decisions, or measure democratic legitimacy.

How social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect

Lorenz, Rauhut, Schweitzer & Helbing · 2011 · PNAS
Peer-reviewed laboratory experiment

In numerical estimation tasks with 144 participants, seeing others’ estimates reduced diversity and increased confidence without a corresponding improvement in collective accuracy.

Limit. An estimation experiment is not a general verdict against discussion; task and interaction structure matter.

Polis: Scaling Deliberation by Mapping High Dimensional Opinion Spaces

Small, Bjorkegren, Erkkilä, Shaw & Megill · 2021 · Recerca
Peer-reviewed system and case study

Describes a system that maps agreement patterns and surfaces statements supported across opinion groups, with real consultation cases.

Limit. A useful map of participants is not a representative estimate of an entire population or evidence that consensus is always desirable.

Komitee Equal Shares: A Hybrid Algorithm for Priceable Participatory Funding

Yang, Scheurer & Helbing · 2026 · CHI Extended Abstracts
Published conference extended abstract · field deployment

Combines personal preferences with group evaluations and allocation receipts. In Winterthur in 2025, 38 citizens allocated CHF 380,000 to 43 projects.

Limit. One deployment, with 32 survey respondents. Simulated comparisons do not isolate every component’s causal contribution.

Human–AI interaction

How people use, question and correct AI assistance during participation.

AI can help humans find common ground in democratic deliberation

Tessler et al. · 2024 · Science
Peer-reviewed experiments

The Habermas Machine uses opinions and critiques to revise group statements. Across experiments with 5,734 participants, AI statements were preferred to those from human mediators.

Limit. Preference for a statement is not proof of factual accuracy, legitimate authority, or lasting political agreement.

Designing Digital Voting Systems for Citizens: Achieving Fairness and Legitimacy in Participatory Budgeting

Yang, Hausladen, Peters, Pournaras, Hänggli Fricker & Helbing · 2024 · Digital Government: Research and Practice
Peer-reviewed empirical article

Studies how voting-system design shapes citizens’ perceptions of fairness and legitimacy in participatory budgeting.

Limit. Perceived fairness and actual understanding are different outcomes; evaluate them separately.

Fairness and Abstraction in Sociotechnical Systems

Selbst, boyd, Friedler, Venkatasubramanian & Vertesi · 2019 · FAT*
Peer-reviewed conceptual paper

Explains how isolating a technical model from its social setting can undermine fairness goals.

Limit. A conceptual analysis of system design, not an empirical guarantee that a particular civic tool is fair.

Synthetic Replacements for Human Survey Data? The Perils of Large Language Models

Bisbee, Clinton, Dorff, Kenkel & Larson · 2024 · Political Analysis
Peer-reviewed empirical audit

Synthetic responses could match survey averages while understating variation and changing statistical relationships. Results were sensitive to prompts and collection dates.

Limit. The study tests particular models and tasks; use its evaluation questions rather than assuming identical failures for every later model.

Complexity science & collective intelligence

How group learning, coordination and judgment emerge from interactions, networks and feedback. Experiments on collective intelligence and social influence sit alongside models of interacting agents.

LLM Agents Grounded in Self-Reports Enable General-Purpose Simulation of Individuals

Park et al. · 2026 revision · arXiv:2411.10109v3
Preprint · June 2026 revision of a 2024 paper

Builds agents from interviews and/or surveys of 1,052 Americans and evaluates predictions on held-out responses against people’s own test–retest consistency.

Limit. Predictive fidelity on studied tasks does not confer consent or political authority. The earlier title was Generative Agent Simulations of 1,000 People.

Belief Engine: Configurable and Inspectable Stance Dynamics in Multi-Agent LLM Deliberation

Yang, Flechtner, Dailisan & Bakker · 2026 · arXiv:2605.15343
Preprint · May 2026

Maintains explicit proposition-level stances with configurable evidence uptake and prior anchoring, recording the evidence associated with updates.

Limit. Evidence scoring and update rules are modeling choices. An inspectable trace is not by itself a faithful simulation of a citizen.
